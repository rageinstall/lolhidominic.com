Okay so, every few weeks the cycle runs. A story about trans people goes viral. The framing is bad. The coverage is worse. By the time a correction or a counter-perspective surfaces, the original post has four million impressions and the response has forty thousand.

Then someone says: "The algorithm is broken."

No. I need people to understand this. The algorithm is not broken. It is doing exactly what it was designed to do.

## Here's what's really happening

Recommendation systems on every major platform are optimized for one thing above everything else: time on app. That's it. The metric is engagement, which in practice means the content that produces the strongest emotional response stays in front of people the longest.

Outrage produces strong emotional responses. Fear produces strong emotional responses. A nuanced, well-sourced correction of a viral misconception produces fewer of those responses, which means it gets less distribution, which means fewer people see it.

This isn't a bug someone forgot to fix. It is the product.

> The way algorithmic amplification works is it doesn't evaluate truth. It evaluates reaction. And reaction to a scary story about trans people is always going to outpace reaction to a calm explainer about why that story is wrong.

## What this means for how you read the feed

The piece of media literacy I keep coming back to is this: the prevalence of a story on your timeline is not evidence of that story's importance. It's evidence that the story is producing engagement.

That's a different thing.

A story can dominate your feed because it's important. It can also dominate your feed because it's enraging, which is useful to platforms regardless of whether it's accurate. Separating those two things is a skill, and it's one that most media literacy education skips over because they're still teaching people to ask "is this source credible?" when the source isn't the problem anymore. Distribution is the problem.

## What to actually do with this

A few things that are more useful than being upset at the algorithm:

**Read before you share.** The click-to-share pipeline on most platforms is too fast. If a story made you feel something strong, that's a reason to slow down, not speed up.

**Check the original.** A lot of viral stories about trans people are about a screenshot of a tweet about an article. Go find the article. Go find what the article actually said. Usually three layers of removal happened between the original source and what you're seeing.

**Note the date.** Old stories recirculate constantly. A policy story from two years ago gets reshared as breaking news. The platform doesn't care. You should.

**Track the correction.** When you see a viral story that later turns out to be wrong or misleading, go look for how the correction was covered. Notice how much smaller that coverage is. File that away.

The goal isn't to opt out of the information environment. The goal is to move through it with your eyes open.

That's the whole thing, actually.
