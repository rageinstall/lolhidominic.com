Real talk: there is no mainstream media story about trans people that I find more consistently misleading than the "both sides" format. And I want to be careful here, because the problem isn't balance itself. Balance is good. The problem is what gets counted as a side.

Let me explain what I mean.

## The structure of the problem

When a major outlet covers a trans policy story, the default format is: here is the thing that is proposed or happening, here is a person who supports it, here is a person who opposes it. Balanced.

Except the "both sides" frame treats the existence of opposition as evidence that a debate exists. It doesn't distinguish between "two groups of people with different policy views" and "a large group of people whose lives are affected by a policy" and "a smaller group of people who disagree with those people existing."

Those aren't equivalent positions, but the coverage treats them as if they are.

> The way this works is it launders fringe positions into the mainstream by giving them equal structural weight to established positions, and it does it in a format that looks like responsible journalism.

## Who this serves

The both-sides frame doesn't serve trans readers. It's not produced for us. It's produced for the median reader who the editor has decided needs to see "all perspectives," which in practice means the reader who isn't trans and needs to be shown that this is a controversy.

For that reader, the format works. They see two sides. They form a both-sides opinion. They feel informed.

For trans readers, the format does something different. It takes the basic question of our right to participate in public life and presents it as unresolved, contested, a matter of ongoing reasonable debate. It treats our existence as a policy position.

## What responsible coverage actually looks like

I'm not asking for advocacy journalism. I'm asking for journalism that distinguishes between different categories of source.

The American Medical Association's position on gender-affirming care is not equivalent to a single politician's objection to it. They're not two sides. They're a body of medical evidence and a political argument. A story that treats them as equivalent weight is not being balanced. It's laundering the political argument as if it has equal epistemic standing to the medical evidence.

That distinction matters. And the way most coverage handles it right now, it gets collapsed.

## This is the part that matters

None of this is accidental. Editors make choices. The both-sides frame is a choice that has consequences for trans people, and those consequences deserve to be named.

The next time you read a trans-related story that feels oddly flat, that feels like it's carefully not saying anything, check whether it's running the both-sides structure. Then check what's being treated as a side.

That's the read.
